The Gallup Park Bridge

A trail bridge over Geddes Pond in Ann Arbor has to be torn down and rebuilt. You run the schedule. Plan it with CPM, buy time with crashing, and test the risk with PERT.

Ann Arbor News / WEMU · October 2024

“New bridge opens in Gallup Park after demolition of aging span in late winter.”

The Gallup Park Bridge, a vital segment of the Border-to-Border Trail crossing Geddes Pond in Ann Arbor, was demolished in early 2024 after engineering assessments identified severe structural deterioration. Construction of the replacement bridge began soon after and continued through the spring and summer, culminating in a public reopening ceremony in October 2024.

The new structure features wider sidewalks, improved safety barriers, and enhanced trail connectivity. It was delivered through close collaboration between the City of Ann Arbor, the Washtenaw County Parks and Recreation Commission, and the University of Michigan, whose faculty and transportation researchers contributed to early planning and design consultations.

Your role

As part of the planning committee, you are now serving as project manager for the Gallup Park Bridge construction. Using CPM, crashing, and PERT, you must plan, monitor, and control the project timeline to ensure:

  • Cost efficiency
  • Minimal disruption to park access and community events

Task 1 of 7 · Network

Draw the AON network

From the case

Draw the AON network.

How to approach it

In an activity-on-node (AON) network, each activity is a node and each arrow is a precedence relation. You can build it straight from the “Immediate predecessors” column of table A.

  1. Draw one node per activity, A through I. Write the normal duration inside.
  2. For every activity, draw an arrow into it from each of its immediate predecessors. F gets three incoming arrows (B, C, E). H gets two (D, F). I gets two (G, H).
  3. Find the start and the finish. Only A has no predecessor and only I has no successor, so the network already has one start and one finish. You don't need separate Start and End nodes.
  4. Mark the burst and merge points. A bursts into four parallel streams. F is the big merge: the truss can only go up once both foundations are done and the steel has arrived.
SolutionHave the class sketch it first, then reveal.

Read it left to right. Site work (A) comes first. Then four streams run in parallel: east piers (B), west piers (C), utility relocation (D) and off-site steel fabrication (E). The truss is erected (F) once B, C and E are done. Deck work (G) and lighting (H) run in parallel. Final inspection and the public opening (I) close the project.

Watch for this

D skips F and feeds H directly. Lighting needs the relocated power and fiber, but the deck does not. Students often draw D → F by mistake.

Task 2 of 7 · CPM

Run the forward and backward pass

From the case

Run CPM (forward/backward) with normal durations. Provide ES, EF, LS, LF, slack; identify critical path(s) and baseline duration.

How to approach it

Forward pass · left to right

ES = largest EF of all immediate predecessors
EF = ES + t

The first activity starts at 0. At a merge, take the max.

Backward pass · right to left

LF = smallest LS of all immediate successors
LS = LF − t

The last activity's LF is the project duration. At a burst, take the min.

Slack

Slack = LS − ES = LF − EF

Activities with zero slack are critical. They form the critical path, the longest path through the network.

Task 3 of 7 · Slack

Where is there room to slip?

From the case

Which activities have slack? Interpret operationally.

How to approach it

Slack (total float) is the number of days an activity can slip without moving the 44-day finish. Read it off the CPM table: any activity with LS − ES > 0 has room. Then ask what that room means on the job site. Who can wait? Where can a delay be absorbed? Which activities need daily attention?

SolutionAsk for the four activities with slack first.

Four activities have slack. Activities with nonzero slack can float without delaying the project. A, E, F, G and I have none.

Schedule at early start, with slack

Critical activity Activity with slack Slack (room to slip)

No slack: A, E, F, G, I

Every day lost on site clearance, steel fabrication, truss erection, deck work or final inspection moves the opening by a day. These are the activities to staff first and watch daily.
Ask the classTo save money, could one foundation crew build both piers, east and then west?

No. Back to back, the piers take 10 + 9 = 19 days starting on day 6, so they would be done on day 25. Erection (F) has to start by day 20 (its LS). The project would slip 5 days, to 49.

Slack is per activity, not a pool you can spend twice. B's 4 days and C's 5 days don't add up to 9 days for one crew.

Task 4 of 7 · Crashing

Buy days only when they pay

From the case

Crashing: You may crash only one activity at a time. Build a step-by-step scorecard and minimize Total Cost = Direct + Overhead × Duration. Note: when multiple paths are critical, duration cannot be shortened by crashing a single activity unless it lies on every critical path.

B. Crashing options

Crashing is linear: each day cut costs the same. Example for E: ($6,360,000 − $5,400,000) ÷ (14 − 10) = $240,000 per day.

How to approach it

  1. Find every critical path at the current durations.
  2. List the activities that lie on every critical path and still have days left to cut. Only these can shorten the project on their own.
  3. Pick the cheapest one and compare its crash cost per day with the $220,000 of overhead a shorter project saves per day.
  4. If it's cheaper, crash it by one day and record the step on the scorecard. If not, stop: no crash saves money.
  5. Recompute the paths and repeat. A crash can turn a near-critical path critical.
Baseline: Direct $28,400,000 + Overhead $220,000 × 44 days ($9,680,000) = $38,080,000

The cost model says you're indifferent, which is why the solution stops at 38 days: there is no positive saving.

A manager might still take the free day as a buffer before a fixed October ceremony. A manager might also decline it. Crashing the final inspection means squeezing safety checks and paying inspectors overtime, and that brings risks the cost table doesn't show.

The case allows one activity at a time. Even if two were allowed, the pair would cost $150,000 + $190,000 = $340,000 for a day that saves only $220,000 of overhead. Crashing F ($200,000) shortens both paths more cheaply because it lies on both.

Task 5 of 7 · PERT

How uncertain is 44 days?

From the case

PERT: compute te and variance per activity; then project μ, σ, and the 99.73% (i.e., 3-sigma value) completion date.

C. PERT three-time estimates

a = optimistic, m = most likely, b = pessimistic (days). PERT assumes each activity time follows a Beta distribution.

How to approach it

  1. For each activity, compute the expected time te = (a + 4m + b) ÷ 6 and the variance σ² = ((b − a) ÷ 6)².
  2. Find the critical path using the expected times te.
  3. Project mean μ = sum of te on the critical path. Project variance = sum of the variances on that path (activity times are assumed independent). σ = √variance.
  4. The completion time is treated as normal, so the 3-sigma (99.73%) date is μ + 3σ.
Worked example, activity E (a = 10, m = 14, b = 20)
te = (10 + 4 × 14 + 20) ÷ 6 = 86 ÷ 6 = 14.33 days
σ² = ((20 − 10) ÷ 6)² = 1.667² = 2.7778

Task 6 of 7 · Confidence check

Can we promise 45 days?

From the case

If leadership requests ≤ 45 working days with 99.73% confidence, check whether your optimal crash plan meets the target.

How to approach it

  1. The 99.73% (3-sigma) completion date is μ + 3σ.
  2. Under the optimal crash plan, the deterministic CPM duration is 38 days. Use it as the mean.
  3. Keep σ = 2.09 days from Task 5. This assumes crashing shortens the schedule but doesn't change how uncertain it is.
  4. The plan meets the target if μ + 3σ ≤ 45.

How much more risk can the plan take?

The target holds as long as σ ≤ (45 − 38) ÷ 3 = 2.33 days. That is only 0.24 days more than today's estimate.

Same question, as a probability

z = (45 − μ) ÷ σ. Baseline: z = 0.24, so P(done by day 45) ≈ 59%. Crash plan: z = 3.35, so P ≈ 99.96%.

Task 7 of 7 · Discussion

What should the project manager take away?

From the case

Managerial insights.

SolutionCollect the class's ideas before revealing.

  1. Recompute the critical path after every crash

    Recomputing the critical path after each crash step ensures crash spending stays efficient and focuses on activities that truly constrain project completion.

    In this case, one day cut from G made A-E-F-H-I critical too. From then on, crashing G again would have wasted $150,000 a day.

  2. The steel truss and the merge at F drive the schedule

    Early off-site steel fabrication (E) and the three-way merge at activity F create long, interdependent chains, making the schedule highly sensitive to disruptions.

    E is the longest activity, sits on the critical path, and carries 64% of the project's variance. A late steel delivery stalls everything at F.

  3. F → G → I is the tail that sets the finish

    The segment F → G → I forms the dominant tail that governs the overall project duration. Every critical path runs through it, and four of the six crash days were bought there (F three times, G once).

  4. A tight deadline needs more than single-activity crashing

    If a strict deadline is set far below the probabilistic three-sigma completion time, single-activity crashing will not suffice. Parallel crashing, overtime or additional crews should be considered to reach the target safely.

  5. Treat near-critical work as critical

    Lighting and electrical (H) has one day of slack, and its path became critical after the first crash. Slack that small gives a manager almost no cushion.

Ask the classThe October ceremony date is fixed. Which single activity would you watch most closely, and what would you do about it?

E, the off-site steel truss. It is the longest activity (14 days), it is critical under both normal and expected times, it feeds the merge at F, and it carries most of the variance. It also happens off-site, so problems surface late.

Possible actions: a weekly progress check at the fabricator, a shop inspection before shipping, and a delivery plan agreed in advance.

Ask the classThe steel is fabricated off-site. Does it really have to wait for site clearance (A)?

If the fabricator could start on day 0, E would finish on day 14 instead of 20. The east piers (done day 16) would then set F's start, and the project would drop to 40 days with no crashing. A-B-F-G-I becomes the critical path. That saves 4 × $220,000 = $880,000 of overhead, far more than the $150,000 the crash plan saves.

The catch: the fabricator may need site survey data or approvals from A before cutting steel. Rethinking a dependency can beat crashing, so it's worth asking what it would take to break this one.

Ask the classIs PERT's 50.76-day estimate optimistic or pessimistic?

Slightly optimistic. PERT only adds up variance along one critical path. A-E-F-H-I averages 43.5 days, just one day behind, so if lighting runs long that path finishes last. Ignoring it understates the risk.

PERT also assumes activity times are independent. A wet spring on Geddes Pond would slow the east piers, the west piers and the deck at the same time.
